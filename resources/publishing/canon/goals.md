# Business goals

Every piece serves the same business. Read this before doing your own job, and never trade these goals for a cleverer line.

## Who Birdcar is

Birdcar is one person, Nick Cannariato, working with clients directly. He finds where work keeps landing on an owner's desk, then builds the tools that let the team handle it without the owner. The work combines operational problem-solving, software delivery, and ongoing care. Birdcar is not an AI agency and not a team: there are no account managers and no handoffs. AI is one possible tool, never the proposition.

## The reader

- The reader is an owner or operator of an established, client-facing service business: an agency, a professional-services firm, or a local service or trade business, roughly 10 to 100 people. The shared problem matters more than the industry.
- The business works. It has customers and good people, and it depends on too much remembering, chasing, copying, and owner intervention.
- Typical moments: considering another admin hire because the team is overloaded; onboarding or follow-up that depends on someone remembering every step; a report assembled by hand from five or more sources; missed follow-ups, late invoices, and failures the owner catches after hours; an employee's departure exposing knowledge that lived in one head.
- DFW, Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, and Louisiana are priorities, but regional relevance is a detail, never the whole identity. Do not sound like a local consultancy.
- The reader is smart, busy, and not an engineer. Assume intelligence, not context. Technology appears only as a consequence for the business and its people. Translate any unavoidable technical word in the same sentence.
- The archive's practitioner audience (support professionals, engineers) is not this reader.

## The offer

- The offer is The Walkthrough (birdcar.dev/walkthrough): free, about an hour, on a video call or in person. Within three business days the owner gets a written report covering what's happening, what I recommend, and where I'd start. The report is theirs to keep, whatever they decide.
- The hour is pitch-free: "If you want to talk about hiring me, you'll bring it up, not me."
- Implementation is a separate purchase. The owner can use the report themselves, hire Nick to implement it, or walk away.
- Fit condition: the Walkthrough works when I can talk to the people doing the work. Otherwise, "I'm not the right fit."

## The one invitation

- Give the whole answer first. The piece fully solves the reader's problem, then makes one plain invitation to The Walkthrough at the end, after the reward.
- Every blog post ends with exactly one invitation and exactly one link to /walkthrough. Derivative pieces (LinkedIn, Bluesky, email) link to the post instead of repeating the invitation.
- Never stack asks (newsletter, share, and book). One ask.
- Name the problem the piece opened with, so the invitation reads as relevant. Use an action verb and what the reader gets. Never "Submit", "Learn more", or "Get started".
- Urgency is consequence copy only: what leaving the problem alone costs. No scarcity, capacity, or deadline language.

## The outcome chain

1. Tools built for the client's work are the how.
2. Better work, faster is the outcome, and it leads.
3. Growing without hiring is the benefit that outcome enables.
4. Healthier margins are the eventual result, and never the headline or a promise.

The approved hero line is "Better work, faster. Without another hire."

## Claims and proof

- No invented savings, revenue, results, testimonials, quotes, or guarantees. No productivity percentages. There are no measured client outcomes yet: say what was built and what was seen, not what it saved.
- Any number in an example scene is plainly hypothetical ("Say you run a 30-person agency..."). Label composite scenes as composites.
- Every statistic carries a named, dated, primary source. If a number does not advance the argument, drop it.
- Approved proof: Craft & Communicate, under that title. Finding performance numbers by hand was part of the agency's reporting work; Nick built a client-facing reporting platform with client management and live-updating data; the agency can offer it to its own customers. No measured results or quotes exist. Never discuss build completion or the retainer.
- Mark a gap in proof `[NEED: proof]`. Mark a personal anecdote only Nick can supply `[TODO: Nick to supply]`. Never fill either with an invention.

## Exclusions

- Never mention WorkOS. Never mention GHX. Never write "DataDash"; the story is "Craft & Communicate".
- GitHub, Heroku, Zapier, Twilio, and Salesforce appear only as work history ("where I've built systems"), never as clients or endorsements. Never name Apple.
- Experience is "fifteen years" or "more than 12 years", never "more than fifteen years".
- Do not borrow anecdotes or numbers from the archive essays as client results.
- No politics. No profanity on anything that sells. No hype, scarcity, guarantees, dollar claims, or AI-startup gloss.
- Work values to hold, and state when relevant as commitments: people over metrics, no employee surveillance, no AI theater, adoption is part of delivery, clients must be able to leave, success is agreed with the client instead of promised as a number.
