# Blog channel

The blog is birdcar.dev/writing, the Writing section of the Birdcar site. It carries Nick's business writing for owners of established, client-facing service businesses: essays and arguments, how-to playbooks, workflow teardowns, and work stories. Each post fully solves the reader's problem, then ends with one plain invitation to The Walkthrough (birdcar.dev/walkthrough).

The archive essays stay exactly as published. Nothing here applies to them, renames them, or re-slugs them. These rules govern new posts only, and they never loosen the public voice: no politics, no profanity on anything that sells, no emoji, no exclamation points, first-person singular only.

## Conflicts and how they resolve

- Narrative-first essays vs answer-first: answer first, then the scene. The scene becomes the evidence for the answer, not the warm-up.
- Argumentative headings vs question headings: mostly question-shaped or plain-claim H2s. One or two voice-led H2s per post are allowed for rhythm.
- Marketing formatting advice (bold key terms, bullet blocks, inline-header lists, Title Case, FAQ blocks, verdict boxes) vs the voice: the voice wins. Put the term in the first words of the sentence instead of bolding it. Use numbered steps only for genuinely sequential actions in playbooks.
- Personal or social edge vs public: public wins. No politics, no profanity on marketing pages.
- Archive practitioner audience vs owners: new posts write for owners. Do not borrow archive anecdotes or numbers.
- Where Nick's own answer-engine research (2026-09-13) conflicts with general marketing advice (llms.txt, artificial chunking, FAQ schema), the research wins.

## Titles

Every post has two titles with two jobs.

- The title is the on-page H1. It carries the voice: a plain claim or a small provocation, specific, sentence case. "Just build it twice" and "Another admin hire won't fix it" are the shape. No listicle numbers, no "Name: explainer" subtitles, no clickbait the post does not pay off.
- The SEO title is for the search result: 50 to 60 characters, phrased the way an owner would search for the problem, primary phrase near the start. "Do you need another admin hire? What to check first" (51) and "How to stop being the bottleneck in your own business" (53) are the shape.
- The meta description is 150 to 160 characters, in the owner's search phrasing, a reason to click and not a summary. Example (158): "When the team is full and the work keeps coming back to you, a new hire often adds a person to the same chores. Here is what to check before you post the job."
- Draft ten title options first, then pick one voice-led title and one search title. Count characters before finishing.
- Suggest a slug of four to six lowercase hyphenated words. Never reuse or alter an archive slug.

## Headings

- Mostly question-shaped or plain-claim H2s in sentence case: "What a Walkthrough report covers", "Why another hire won't fix it", "Who owns the Friday report when its author is out?"
- One or two voice-led H2s are allowed for rhythm, such as "Nobody decided to work this way".
- Never label headings (Introduction, Conclusion, FAQ, Pros, Cons, Verdict, Overview, Summary).
- A clean order: H2 under the title, H3 only under an H2, no skipped levels. Use H3 sparingly.
- The H2 that matches the post's target query uses the owner's phrasing for that query.

## Openings

Answer first, then the scene.

1. The first sentence states the claim or answer in one plain sentence an owner would quote. The site has no separate dek field, so this is the first sentence under the title.
2. The first paragraph finishes the answer in 40 to 60 words, with the subject named (no "this" or "it" as the opening noun) and the target phrase within the first 100 words.
3. Then the recognizable scene: a moment at work, a repeated chore, a specific observation. Use second person or a flagged hypothetical ("Say you run a 30-person agency...").
4. Then the argument earns the rest.

Never open with a definition, "In this post", an era opener ("In today's..."), company history, or a credentials paragraph. If the post is about a term, define it in the first paragraph in one sentence.

## Section structure

- Under every H2, the first sentence answers the heading in plain prose with the subject named, so the passage stands alone when extracted.
- One idea per paragraph, with a subject-first opening sentence. Do not shorten paragraphs to a fixed size and do not add summary boxes. Narrative paragraphs run 3 to 6 sentences; a one- or two-sentence paragraph lands a point.
- Run hook, retain, reward at section level: the heading and first line raise the question, the middle keeps it open with a scene or a step, the end pays it off.
- Alternate narrative and analytical sections. Never stack three analytical sections.
- Define any term the post coins or leans on, in one sentence, where it first appears. Use the pattern names from the "Eight ways work gets stuck" page when they fit (for example "Waiting on your yes", "It runs on remembering", "Assembled by hand", "The same information, typed twice").
- State the limit early: one sentence on what this will not fix.
- Answer a recurring objection in its own question H2, in prose. No FAQ section and no "short answer" box.
- Switch perspective on purpose: second person for scenes, first person for judgment and method.
- Closing: end on the last concrete point, then the invitation paragraph. No recap, no inspirational line, no mirror of the opening.
- Carry over the narrative craft: the three sentence modes (subordinating for analysis, additive for scenes, satiric for calling out a bad idea, never a person), the turn at section boundaries, weighted first and last sentences, form that enacts content, and a refrain only when it earns its place.

## Answer-engine and search rules

- Answer-first passages: the claim opens every section and the first passage is 40 to 60 words with the subject named. One target query per post.
- Sources: every statistic carries a named, dated, primary source, in the pattern "According to [source] ([date]), [fact]", then one sentence of implication. Cite original research, not summaries. If a number does not advance the argument, drop it. Never repeat vendor or study percentages about answer-engine visibility as fact.
- Prefer Nick's own firsthand specifics. Mark gaps `[NEED: proof]`. Never invent proof.
- Author and dates: the byline is Nick Cannariato, the date is the original publish date, and a "Last updated" date appears only when the edit is real.
- Internal links: two or three related posts with descriptive anchors (not "click here"). Link the "Eight ways work gets stuck" page where a pattern is named. The post has exactly one link to /walkthrough, in the invitation.
- External links: cite authoritative primary sources for claims.
- No self-ranked "best of" listicles. A comparison, if one is warranted, is an honest essay with dated facts that says who should pick the other option.
- No gating: no email wall, no PDF-only content. The most useful material stays open.
- No artificial chunking, no separate version "for AI", no mass-produced posts from fan-out queries. Use fan-out only to find sub-questions this one post should answer.
- No FAQ section and no FAQ markup on posts. FAQ blocks belong to the Walkthrough and offer pages.
- Structured data is handled by the site and must match visible facts. The draft supplies an accurate title, description, date, and author, and claims nothing the page does not show.
- Images and charts: descriptive file names and alt text, and a prose equivalent for every chart.
- The primary phrase appears in the SEO title, the first 100 words, and one question H2. No stuffing.
- Nothing here promises ranking, citation, or recommendation.

## Invitation rules

- One invitation per post, at the end, after the reward. Never before it.
- It is a plain-language invitation to The Walkthrough (birdcar.dev/walkthrough): about an hour, free, a written report within three business days that includes where I would start, yours to keep. Say the hour is pitch-free ("If you want to talk about hiring me, you'll bring it up, not me").
- Name the exact problem the post opened with, so the invitation reads as relevant. Use an action verb and what the reader gets. Never "Submit", "Learn more", or "Get started".
- One honest absence, stated once ("no implementation purchase required"). Do not stack negations.
- Do not stack asks (newsletter, share, and book). One ask.
- Urgency is consequence copy only. No scarcity, capacity, or deadline language.
- State the fit condition when relevant: the Walkthrough works when I can talk to the people doing the work.

## Examples

> A new admin hire fixes a team that is short on hands. It does not fix a team that is short on a process, and from the owner's desk the two look identical. Before you post the job, find out which one you have.

> ## Why another hire won't fix it
>
> Another hire adds a person to the same chores. The follow-up still depends on someone remembering it, the Friday report still takes an afternoon, and the person who knows how the quarterly filing works is now training a second person to remember it.

> ## What a Walkthrough report covers
>
> A Walkthrough report covers three things: what is happening in the work, what I recommend changing, and where I would start. The first change comes first for a reason, and the report says why.
