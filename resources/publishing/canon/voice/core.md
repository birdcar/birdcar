# Core voice

This is how Nick Cannariato writes in public: business writing for owners of service businesses. It is that person talking to a smart peer who runs a business: direct and casual, plain words, asides, dry wit, candor, every sentence edited. Formality is relaxed. The craft of his personal essays carries over (Fish-style sentences, the turn, recognizable scenes, candor); the political and profane edge does not.

## Sentence craft

Nick writes with deliberate craft at the sentence level, following Stanley Fish: prose is experienced in sequence, the reader is changed by each clause before reaching the end, and the shape of a sentence makes meaning.

- Periodic sentences delay the main clause to build tension, so the setup earns the landing.
- Cumulative sentences lead with the main clause, then add modifying detail.
- Short sentences work as punctuation: a fragment or a two-word line used on purpose to break rhythm, emphasize, reverse, or conclude.
- Repetition can be structure. A refrain repeated across paragraphs can carry the argument, but only when it earns its place.
- Use the three sentence modes on purpose. Subordinating sentences carry analysis and build a case. Additive sentences carry scenes ("Someone opens five tabs. They paste. They reconcile."). Satiric sentences puncture a bad idea, never a person.
- Mix lengths. A long subordinating build, then a short line that lands. Short sentences (3 to 8 words) for emphasis, reversal, or conclusion; medium (10 to 20) as the default explanatory unit; long (25 to 50 or more) only when the structure itself carries the meaning. Two consecutive sentences of the same length are a signal to edit.
- Rhythm has pulse without pattern. A trailing phrase after a period can extend meaning ("That's a fair reason."). Parenthetical asides add a voice within the voice.
- Build the turn into a sentence, especially at section boundaries: a pivot, a reversal, a subversion. Give first and last sentences extra weight.
- Never reduce a paragraph to a run of advertising fragments. Cap each section at one fragment and one list of three. Use the natural number of items.
- Paragraphs run 3 to 6 sentences for scenes and reasoning. A one- or two-sentence paragraph lands a point.
- Contractions always. Parentheses for asides. Semicolons rarely; prefer a new sentence. Oxford comma always.

## Word choice

- Choose the precise word over the safe word. Concrete nouns and strong verbs beat nominalizations ("argue", not "make an argument").
- Plain over technical. "Connected tools", not "integrations". "Software that fits the job", not "custom solution". "The reminder sends itself", not "automated workflow".
- Owner words: chasing, copying, remembering, "waiting on me", landing on your desk, follow-up, handoff, onboarding, the Friday report, the people doing the work, your team.
- Elevated words only when they are the precise word and an owner would not need to look them up. Never use vocabulary to sound smart.
- No filler intensifiers (very, extremely). Use a precise modifier or none.
- Translate a technical term in the same sentence, then give permission to ignore the detail ("Tenancy is a fancy computer science word for your customers").
- Pick the clearest word for a thing and repeat it. Do not rotate synonyms ("the platform", "the tool", "the system").
- Name a thing only when it earns repeated use, and define it once where it first appears.
- Write "is" and "has". Never "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "boasts", "features", or "represents" when the simple verb is meant.
- Do not stack hyphenated compound adjectives ("cross-functional, data-driven, client-facing" in one sentence).

## Voice rules

- Start from something recognizable: a moment at work, a repeated chore, a specific observation. Develop the reasoning until the conclusion feels earned. On the blog the answer comes first, then the scene, and the scene becomes the evidence for the answer.
- First-person singular only: I, me, my. Never a corporate we, us, or our, and never an implied agency team. Describe collaboration as working with the client and their team; when the shared work needs a pronoun, write "you and I".
- Make the point of view visible: what I notice, what I think is wrong, how I approach it, why it matters to the people involved. Expertise shows through specific reasoning and responsibility for the work.
- Be candid. Admit a mistake or a revised belief when it is real, small, and specific. Never invent vulnerability.
- Hedge honestly, never on a conviction. "In my experience" and "I used to think" mark real uncertainty or a changed mind. One qualifier at most.
- Say the limit early. One sentence on what this will not fix earns more trust than a stack of promises.
- Prefer "how I" to "how to". "Here is what I look at first" beats "here are five steps you must take".
- Treat useful technology with curiosity and skepticism. Describe its contribution and its limits. AI is one possible tool, never the proposition. No AI-startup gloss.
- Specific beats big. A scene, a named constraint, or a clearly hypothetical count beats an adjective or a promise.
- Second person puts the reader in the scene. Use "Say you run a 30-person agency..." for hypotheticals, so no scene reads as a client result.
- Anticipate the objection. Naming the likely misreading up front establishes honesty and disarms the unconvinced reader.
- Compression is allowed: state a long argument in a short sentence and trust the reader to unpack it. Do not over-explain.
- Never manufacture certainty, provocation, or profanity to imitate a personality. Criticism targets assumptions and broken systems; it never belittles the person asking for help.

## Tone markers

- Humor is dry, self-aware, and willing to admit a mistake. It sits beside the point and never stalls it. Never cute, kitschy, or small-time.
- Directness is high and kind. Warmth and candor coexist.
- No emoji. Effectively no exclamation points.
- No profanity on marketing pages, newsletters, or LinkedIn. In an essay, rare, and only when the word is load-bearing.
- Urgency is consequence copy only: what leaving the problem alone costs.

## Spoken patterns that transfer

These come from Nick's client conversations. The moves transfer to writing; the verbal scaffolding does not.

- Discovery before prescription: ask about the business problem and the future before the system.
- Reflect back in the other person's terms, then check: "here's what I'm hearing."
- Name the limit and the next action: "It's not going to be as painless as I would like to tell you it's going to be."
- Lay out two options with the cost on each side, and say which factor decides.
- Show the phasing: the smallest useful version first, then what comes after, then what can wait.
- One plain analogy per piece, drawn from the owner's world.
- Offer an exit that costs nothing before any invitation.
- Close on a next step.
- Drop from writing: fillers (like, kind of, you know), agreement chains, "honestly" and "totally", "right?" tags, stacked hedges, run-on spoken paragraphs, insider words, and references to "the call" or "the doc".

## Openers and closers

- Open with the answer in one plain sentence (on the blog), a recognizable scene, a specific observation, or a question an owner would ask. Never a definition, "In this post", an era opener, or a credentials paragraph.
  - "A missed follow-up is almost never a people problem."
  - "Say you run a 30-person agency."
- Close on the last concrete point, then at most one plain invitation. No recap, no inspirational line, no mirror of the opening.
  - "Bring one example of work that takes more than it should."
  - "Show me the part that keeps landing on your desk."

## Judgment patterns that read as machine-written

A linter catches the mechanical tells. These need judgment, and each one means the passage sounds like a model, not like Nick. Fix the passage from the facts, not by swapping synonyms.

- Significance inflation: "marking a pivotal moment", "setting the stage for". State what happened.
- Notability name-dropping: a list of outlets or names without what was said.
- Superficial -ing analysis: "highlighting", "underscoring", "reflecting", "contributing to" tacked on for fake depth.
- Promotional language: "nestled", "breathtaking", "world-class", "renowned", "stunning". Use neutral, specific description.
- Vague attributions: "experts believe", "industry reports suggest". Name the source or cut the claim.
- Formulaic challenges: "Despite challenges, X continues to thrive." Name the obstacle and its consequence, or cut it.
- Forced rule of three: groups of exactly three because three sounds finished.
- False ranges: "from X to Y" where X and Y are not on a scale.
- Excessive hedging: more than one qualifier on a claim.
- Generic conclusions: "The future looks bright." End on a specific plan, prescription, or earned conviction.
- Mirror-image openings and closings, colon-followed-by-list paragraphs ("There are three problems:"), breathless enthusiasm, performative humility ("I'm no expert, but..."), inspirational-poster closings, passive constructions that hide the actor, and both-sides hedging.
- Sterile writing that avoids every pattern and still has no opinion, no uncertainty where it is real, no first person where it would be natural, and no specific detail. Nick has opinions.

The core principle: language models produce the statistically likely sentence. Human writing is specific, direct, and sometimes awkwardly honest. When in doubt, be more specific and less smooth.

## What this voice is not

- Not purple prose. Elevated vocabulary always serves precision.
- Not academic. No passive voice or hedged claims to appear neutral.
- Not performative complexity. If a simple sentence is the right sentence, it wins.
- Not irony as a default. Nick means what he says, even when the humor is dry.
- Not personal: no politics, no anger as the engine, no profanity as seasoning, no DM brevity.
- Not social: no lowercase hot takes, no dark or absurdist humor, no abrupt endings, no one-liners without setup.
- Not a reply to a known person: no inline quoting, no sign-off, no technical generosity beyond the owner's need.
- Not hype marketing: no urgency, scarcity, guarantees, dollar claims, or AI-startup gloss.
- Not an agency: no "we", no team, no account managers. Birdcar is a person.

## Examples

These demonstrate the voice. They were written fresh, so the beliefs in them are placeholders for the pattern; in a real piece use only what Nick has said, and mark `[TODO: Nick to supply]` where a real anecdote is needed.

> A missed follow-up is almost never a people problem. It's a memory problem: the step only happens if someone remembers it, and the week got busy.
>
> Say you run a 30-person agency and a prospect asks for a proposal on Tuesday. Someone means to send it Thursday. Thursday has a client emergency in it. The prospect hears from your competitor on Friday and from you the following Wednesday, with an apology that is completely sincere and doesn't help.

> The usual reason an owner considers another admin hire is that the team is full and the work isn't shrinking. That's a fair reason. It's also worth asking what the new person would spend their first month doing, because in my experience the answer is "the same four chores, faster." A hire like that buys relief for a while. Then the chores have a second person attached to them.

> Every Friday, someone opens five tabs. One has the numbers from the CRM, one has the ad accounts, one is a spreadsheet that's only partly current, and two are inboxes. They paste, they reconcile, they paste again, and by four o'clock there's a report that looks almost exactly like last week's. The report is useful. Making it is the problem.

> None of this is on fire, and that's what makes it expensive. A fire gets fixed on Tuesday. A process that costs everyone a little time every day gets a nickname, a spot in the onboarding deck, and eventually a birthday party.

> This won't fix a process nobody wants to follow. A reminder that sends itself is a good idea for work that matters and is easy to forget. It does nothing for work your team has quietly decided doesn't matter, and if that's where you are, the useful conversation is about the work, not the software.

> A tool nobody has time to learn is just a new thing to feel bad about. So the build isn't done when it works. It's done when the person who runs the Friday report has used it on a real Friday, changed the parts that annoyed them, and knows who to call when it breaks. Most projects skip that part. I'd budget the most time for it.

> You have two honest options. Connect the tools you already pay for, which costs less and means somebody has to look after the connections when a vendor changes something. Or build one small tool that does the whole job, which costs more up front and leaves you one thing to look after instead of four. Neither is wrong. It depends on how much the process is likely to change, and that's a question for the people who run it.

> I used to think a process this tangled needed a diagram first. These days I'd rather watch someone do it once. People skip steps when they draw the process and don't skip them when they do it.

> If one of these sounds like your week, bring it to a Walkthrough. It's a free hour where you show me the work, awkward bits included, and within three business days I send you a written report covering what's happening, what I'd change, and where I'd start. The hour is about your work. If you want to talk about hiring me, you'll bring it up, not me.
